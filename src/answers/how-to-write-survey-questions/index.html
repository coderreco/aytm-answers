How to write survey questions: a practical guide

Last updated: 2025-11-14

TL;DR: Great survey questions are clear, unbiased, specific, and easy for respondents to answer. Avoid double-barreled wording, unclear timeframes, leading language, jargon, and unnecessarily long lists. Write with the respondent in mind.

The fundamentals of strong survey questions

Strong questions are:

  • Clear — easy to understand on the first read
  • Specific — no ambiguity
  • Unbiased — neutral language
  • Answerable — not demanding impossible recall
  • Single-focused — only one idea per question

Common question types

  • Closed-ended: ratings, multiple choice, grids, MaxDiff
  • Open-ended: text responses for deeper insight
  • Behavioral: past actions vs. hypothetical intent
  • Attitudinal: beliefs, perceptions, satisfaction

Best practices for writing survey questions

1. Avoid double-barreled questions

Bad: “How satisfied are you with the price and features?” Good: Ask about each separately.

2. Avoid leading or biased wording

Bad: “How much do you love our amazing new feature?” Good: “How appealing is this feature?”

3. Define timeframes clearly

Bad: “How often do you buy this?” Good: “How many times have you purchased this in the past 3 months?”

4. Keep answer options mutually exclusive and exhaustive

Use ranges, “Other,” and “Not sure” when appropriate.

5. Use simple, conversational language

Avoid jargon, acronyms, or internal terminology.

6. Put sensitive questions later

Demographics or income questions work best near the end.

Designing your answer lists

  • Balance scales (even number vs. odd number of points)
  • Keep lists similar in length and structure
  • Randomize lists when bias is a concern
  • Use labels for each point on long scales

Testing and refining questions

  • Run a soft launch to check early data
  • Look for confusion patterns
  • Check open-ends for misunderstanding
  • Shorten or simplify questions that slow respondents down

FAQs

How many questions should a survey have?

10–20 is typical for general surveys; longer studies work if well designed.

Where should open-ended questions go?

Use them selectively—often near the end of modules or for diagnostics.

Should we always randomize answer lists?

Randomize when order bias could influence results; otherwise keep lists stable for tracking.
