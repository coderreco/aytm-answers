Concept testing: how to design and run effective studies

Last updated: 2025-11-14

TL;DR: Concept testing helps you quickly evaluate new ideas for products, services, or campaigns before you invest heavily in them. Write clear, comparable concepts, show them to the right audience, and use a mix of closed- and open-ended questions to understand appeal, relevance, and improvement opportunities.

What is concept testing?

Concept testing is the process of showing ideas to your target audience and asking for structured feedback. A “concept” can be:

  • A new product or feature idea
  • A value proposition or positioning statement
  • A service offering or bundle
  • An early-stage campaign or message platform

The goal is to learn which ideas resonate, with whom, and why—before making final decisions.

When to run a concept test

Concept tests are useful when you need to:

  • Screen many ideas down to a smaller set
  • Compare variations of a single idea (e.g., price points, feature sets)
  • Find which concepts have the highest potential for your target
  • Refine language, benefits, and proof points

How to write strong concepts

Good concept stimuli are:

  • Clear: respondents should understand what is being offered and for whom.
  • Comparable: each concept should follow a similar structure so differences in performance are meaningful.
  • Grounded: claims should be believable and aligned with what you can deliver.
  • Focused: each concept should represent a distinct idea, not a mix of multiple directions.

A simple structure is:

  • Headline: the core promise or big idea
  • Support paragraph: what it is, how it works, who it is for
  • Key benefits: a short bullet list of main advantages
  • Proof points: any credibility elements you want to test (data, endorsements, technology, etc.)

Monadic vs. comparison designs

On AYTM, you can set up different design approaches depending on your needs:

  • Monadic: each respondent sees just one concept. This reduces bias and is best when you have fully formed ideas.
  • Sequential monadic: respondents see more than one concept, but each is rated independently with reset questions.
  • Comparative: respondents see multiple concepts side-by-side and pick the best. This is efficient but increases bias and contrast effects.

For high-stakes decisions or subtle differences, monadic or sequential monadic designs are usually preferred.

Key questions to include in a concept test

Typical question areas include:

  • Overall appeal: how appealing or interesting is this idea?
  • Relevance: how well does it fit respondent needs or situations?
  • Uniqueness: how different does it feel from current options?
  • Purchase or usage intent: how likely are they to use or buy if available?
  • Perceived value: if pricing is included, how fair or expensive it feels.
  • Fit with brand: for branded concepts, how well it matches expectations.
  • Open-ended feedback: what they like most, least, and what they would change.

Interpreting concept test results

When you analyze results, focus on:

  • Topline performance: which concepts are strongest on appeal and intent?
  • Segment differences: which audiences prefer which concepts?
  • Drivers: which attributes and benefits correlate with higher intent?
  • Verbatims: open-ended comments that reveal why people feel the way they do.

The combination of scores and verbatims shows not just which concepts win, but how to improve them.

Common pitfalls to avoid

  • Overloading respondents: too many concepts or too much text can cause fatigue.
  • Vague concepts: if respondents don’t understand the idea, scores are hard to interpret.
  • Ignoring base sizes: make sure segment comparisons have enough respondents.
  • Chasing small differences: don’t over-interpret tiny score gaps without a clear story.

FAQs

How many concepts can I test at once?

It depends on your design. Monadic tests often include 2–6 concepts across different cells. Comparative designs can show more, but watch for fatigue and context effects.

Do concepts need to be final?

No. Concept testing is especially powerful early, when changes are still easy to make. Rough but clear concepts are better than detailed but inflexible ones.

How do I choose a winner?

Look at performance on your primary decision metric (often purchase intent or appeal) and cross-check with supporting measures and verbatims. The “winner” should not only score well but also have a strong, believable path to execution.
